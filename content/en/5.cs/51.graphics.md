## Basic Components

### Vertex

A vertex is a point in 3D space, defined by its x, y, and z coordinates. Multiple vertices are used to create polygons or meshes that form the geometry of 3D objects.

### Rasterization

The process of converting 3D models into a 2D image by mapping the 3D scene onto a 2D viewport (like your screen). This step converts polygons into pixels or fragments.

### Shaders

Shaders are small programs that run on the GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) to control how vertices and pixels are processed and rendered.

- Vertex Shader: Transforms 3D coordinates into 2D screen coordinates
- Fragment/Pixel Shader: Determines the color and appearance of individual pixels

### Texture

Textures are images applied to the surface of a geometry to give it more detail or realism (e.g., brick textures on a wall). You can use textures for color, bump mapping, normal mapping (purple-looking texture) and other techniques to create the illusion of depth.

#### Diffuse/Albedo map

The default base color map on mainstream graphics.
#### Blinn-Phone

Legacy simpler way of doing shininess on limited computation environment.

#### PBR Texture (Physically Based Rendering)

In code, these are typically numbers between 0.0 and 1.0.

| Properties | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Metallic (M) | Don't diffuse color; they only reflect |
| Roughness (R) | A smooth surface have microfacets all pointing the same way (mirror). A rough surface (R=1) has facets pointing in random directions, scattering light into a blurry "highlight |
| Wetness (W) | A thin layer of water on top of a surface. It creates a secondary, perfectly smooth reflection (R = 0) on top of the original material, while darkening the base color (albedo) because water traps some light. |

### Rendering Equation

$L_o(p, \omega_o) = \int_{\Omega} f_r(p, \omega_i, \omega_o) L_i(p, \omega_i) (n \cdot \omega_i) d\omega_i$

- Outgoing Light, $L_o(p, \omega_o)$: The total light leaving point $p$ in the direction of your eye ($\omega_o$). This is the final color of the pixel you see.

- The Integral, $\int_{\Omega} \dots d\omega_i$: This sum up everything from every direction in the hemisphere ($\Omega$)

- Incoming Light, $L_i(p, \omega_i)$: The amount of light arriving at point $p$ from a specific direction ($\omega_i$)

- The BRDF, $f_r(p, \omega_i, \omega_o)$: Material Filter (metalness, roughness, wetness)

- The Cosine Law, $(n \cdot \omega_i)$: This accounts for the angle. If it hits at a shallow grazing angle, the energy is spread out and the surface looks dimmer.

### Path Tracing

A form of ray tracing, where light rays are simulated from eyes, reflecting multiple times based on the tracing conditions until a light source. This is very computationally expensive, but produces realistic results.
